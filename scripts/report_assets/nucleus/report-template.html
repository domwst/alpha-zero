The 0.95 cutoff changes choices in one game in five

This is an offline counterfactual filter, not a strength experiment. It identifies moves that would have been unavailable at historical positions. It does not establish that those moves were blunders or predict the resulting winners.

Many tiny options, little total probability. At top-p 0.95, the mean support shrinks from 173.8 to 61.1 moves. Removal counts vary sharply: the median is six moves, and the 90th percentile is 342. The filter excludes 3,925 of 373,712 recoverable played actions (1.05%).

Sampling often becomes greedy. In both the moves 13–20 and moves 21+ groups, the median retained set has one move. The first six moves retain a median of 28.5; a few broad policies pull the overall mean upward.

Seats are affected differently. Top-p 0.95 excludes 0.85% of first-player actions and 1.25% of second-player actions. This reflects different historical positions, not proof of an unfair filtering rule.

Next step: inspect a representative sample of excluded and retained alternatives using stronger search before selecting a cutoff or starting another training experiment.

Method, interpretation and provenance

Dataset. Replay checkpoints 60–69 contained 40,000 game entries. SHA-256 deduplication of complete serialized games removed 22,500 duplicate entries, leaving 17,500 games and 391,212 positions before symmetry augmentation.

Historical sampling policy. These archived targets already contain the old collector’s temperature transform. The audit normalizes floating-point rounding only, then applies top-p. It does not apply temperature twice or project results under the new paired 0.7 schedule.

Filtering. Sort positive-probability moves, retain the shortest prefix reaching top-p, and extend through every exact tie at the boundary. This often removes less than 1 − top-p probability mass. Renormalize the retained distribution.

Actions. Recover the played move from consecutive board states and verify it with the game transition function, including the player flip. Terminal boards are absent, leaving 373,712 recoverable actions. Once an action changes in a real game, later positions can diverge; this audit does not simulate those continuations.

What this suggests. Tail filtering is consequential enough to investigate, but excluded moves can be reasonable alternatives. Whole-game outcomes cannot assign credit to individual actions. Inspecting search values and tactical consequences is the next useful check.

Policy-surprise weighting. For a fixed search target π, cross-entropy and KL divergence have the same model gradient:

CE(π, pθ) = H(π) + KL(π ‖ pθ)

H(π) is constant with respect to model parameters, but varies between positions. A fixed surprise weight adds prioritization between samples; it is not an independent learning signal. KataGo’s method uses collection-time policy surprise to change sampling frequency. No such weighting was enabled here.

Validation. The histogram pass reproduces the original audit’s position counts, played-action exclusions, mean support and mean removed mass at every cutoff. Each histogram sums to its group’s position count. Joint before/after counts reproduce both marginal histograms and the removed-move histogram; every unfiltered pair lies on the diagonal. Optimized cutoffs are checked against the original filter on sampled rows; top-p 1.0 preserves all actions.
